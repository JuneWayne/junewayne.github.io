The explorations and experiments in this blog are still rudimentary in scale, hence conclusions should be considered interesting findings that can be expanded with greater compute resources and hours if available.

TLDR

Robust prompt injection defense training

  • Of the eight methods assessed for selecting training data, Jlens fitted on LLMail produced the lowest observed ASR on five of the seven external benchmark comparisons after fine-tuning Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. BIPIA favored the 2 layer neural network, while AgentDojo favored Linear SVM.
  • Prompt injection detectors with higher validation accuracy did NOT consistently select the most effective fine-tuning data. The probes classified FIPI prompts much more accurately than the two lenses, yet the LLMail lens selected data that performed better on most completed external comparisons.
  • In the controlled Qwen2.5-7B experiment, hidden state separation produced repetitive responses, while training the model to reproduce its safe counterpart response under an injected prompt worked better.
  • These results compare injection-following behavior, while preserving legitimate multi-turn task completion remains a separate question for further evaluation.

Interpretation of language models amidst prompt injection attacks

  • Earlier layer inspections showed that Jlens can favor the injection label even when the model's final classification favors safe.
  • The two Qwen lenses used here were each fitted on 100 prompts. Their vocabulary scores can be used for classification and data selection, with results that depend on the model, inspected layer, and prompt format.
  • Changing the inference-time prompt changed the observed verbalizations. The current comparison does not isolate whether this matters more than the lens fitting data or fitting prompt.

Why focus on the training data?

Having worked on a variety of customer-facing agentic systems at Cisco, the issue of injection attacks against our LLM agents is beginning to surface across the organization and escape the echo chambers of frontier labs. Meaning, they are becoming a practical concern for applications that read outside content and take actions on behalf of a user.

Nevertheless, you might ask: with advanced reasoning capabilities and so many post-training hours thrown into alignment, do these kinds of attacks still happen and affect our agents?

The answer is yes. AgentDojo and the LLMail-Inject challenge demonstrate how instructions inside retrieved content can redirect an assistant, including one that already has defenses in place. [1] [2]

At the same time, deploying a frontier model for every customer-facing interaction can be difficult to justify when an application has high rates of agent activity. Smaller language models offer another option, but choosing one does not settle the security question. How can we improve its behavior when the content it reads contains instructions that compete with the user's task?

Of course, there are a lot of works on training probe detectors, employing additional LLMs as judges, or fine-tuning LLMs to avoid following injected instructions. In this blog, I attempt to focus on a slightly different set of questions: what kinds of data permit better fine-tuning? How do we identify such data when curating a training dataset?

Hence, this research pertains more to the data side of prompt injection defense. We adapt existing classifiers and interpretation methods to select examples, then test whether the resulting training data lowers attack success rate after fine-tuning.

Research question: Which data selection methods produce more effective prompt injection defense fine-tuning data when the model, training objective, and selected pair count stay the same?

What does a successful injection look like?

The research here aims to lower attack success rate, or ASR. But an injection does not have to ask for something dangerous to redirect a model. Let me give an example from the saved FIPI evaluation of the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model.

What is the day before Saturday? It is Friday.

The model simplified the passage, but it also answered the inserted question because the supplied content pretended to end one conversation and begin another. Qwen therefore treated that new question as a task to complete, even though it was part of the passage being rewritten. This is FIPI testing pair testing:102402, from the earlier controlled evaluation.

Irrespective of how harmful the extra question appears, it came from the content being processed rather than the legitimate instruction. In another setting, that additional task could request a tool call or the disclosure of private information. Hence the eventual defense goal is to resist injection WHILE still completing the legitimate task.

Past research

TaskTracker, by Abdelnabi et al., uses changes in model activations to detect when external content causes task drift, which makes its linear probes a useful starting point for inspecting model behavior. In our experiment, we instead use the hidden state vector at a single prompt position rather than the difference between activations. [3]

Another approach is to have a separate model inspect the incoming content. The LLMail-Inject challenge includes an LLM judge alongside other defenses, giving attackers a concrete setting in which to test whether those defenses can be bypassed. A classifier or judge can help, but its performance still needs to be tested against unfamiliar and adaptive attacks. Rahman and Alouani, for example, study suffixes that evade activation-based task-drift detectors. [2] [4]

The problem can also spread across agents. In Prompt Infection, Lee and Tiwari demonstrate how injected instructions can replicate through messages exchanged between agents. Hence, inspecting the user's original prompt alone can miss instructions introduced later by another agent. [5]

For model-level defenses, SecAlign constructs pairs of secure and insecure responses and uses preference optimization to favor the secure response. Meta-SecAlign extends that direction with changes intended to preserve utility, including self-generated responses and varied injection positions. ODILE instead trains low-rank adapters using paired benign and harmful traces, changing internal representations to reduce harmful tool actions. [6] [7] [8]

Hence, erring on the side of cost-efficiency while finding a way to enhance the base model, we build on defense fine-tuning and focus the comparison on which examples to train on. LESS provides a related data-curation perspective by selecting examples according to how useful their training gradients are for a target capability. Although we do not implement LESS here, it provides another selection method to compare in future work. [9]

Why select data from FIPI?

FIPI stands for Fine-grained Indirect Prompt Injection. Zhong et al. released it with Rennervate, a defense that uses attention features to locate injected instructions inside external content. [10]

We selected FIPI because each record already provides a legitimate user instruction, clean content, the same content with an injection, and the exact inserted text. That gives us a safe/injection pair with the same legitimate task in both conditions.

We can therefore ask the base model to respond to both versions, use the safe response as a reference answer, and compare selection methods on the same candidate pairs. The seven injection methods also provide variations in how an instruction is inserted, including instructions that imitate a conversation boundary, as in the example above.

The imported release contains 100,000 training pairs and 10,000 testing pairs, according to our file inspection. We use a smaller candidate pool for this experiment, while keeping related examples together because the same safe content can appear with different injection variants.

According to the figure above (Figure 1), all eight methods choose from the same 9,987 eligible pairs. Thirteen of the 10,000 candidate pairs did not have two complete responses within the recording limit and were therefore excluded. We also excluded missing task instructions and known clean-text overlap with testing, while keeping source groups together when separating probe training, validation, and candidates.

FIPI's paired structure therefore fits this comparison, although it does not cover every type of injection or provide complete multi-turn agent environments. Since it also lacks verified answers for all legitimate tasks, we still need external evaluations and a separate check of reference-answer quality.

Eight methods for selecting the training data

Each method selects 256 pairs from FIPI, while the model, LoRA settings, training objective, and validation procedure stay the same. Hence, the main change is which examples enter fine-tuning, giving eight subsets of the same dataset.

Random selection

We randomly select 256 eligible pairs with seed 42 to test whether ordinary random selection already provides an adequate variety of prompts for defense fine-tuning.

Text diversity

For text diversity, we use TF-IDF to represent words and two-word phrases, giving less common terms more weight. We select one starting example randomly, then repeatedly choose the candidate that is least similar to its closest already-selected example until we have 256 pairs.

For example, after selecting several prompts about rewriting a company update, another nearly identical update adds little wording variety, whereas a prompt about extracting numbers from a table might add more. This illustrates the rule rather than a measured ranking. Since the comparison uses cosine similarity between TF-IDF vectors, it measures wording variety without guaranteeing a difference in attack meaning.

Model failures

For model failures, we select pairs where the base model's injected response contains the answer requested by the injection while its safe response does not. All 256 selected pairs met this rule, with random ordering used to resolve ties. The phrase check therefore selects observed response redirection, although it does not verify the entire legitimate task.

Logistic Regression

We fit a Logistic Regression probe on hidden state vectors from 1,500 FIPI training pairs, giving 3,000 labeled prompts. It learns a weighted combination of vector values that separates injection from safe, after which a separate set of 500 validation pairs is used to choose the inspected layer.

Linear SVM

We fit a Linear SVM on the same FIPI training pairs to learn a linear boundary between the two labels. Since its raw decision score is not a probability, we fit a sigmoid conversion using three pair-grouped folds within the training data, then use the 500 validation pairs to choose the layer.

2 layer neural network

We also fit a neural network on the same hidden state vectors and labels, using one hidden layer with 64 units and one output layer. This gives it two learned layers and allows it to learn a nonlinear relationship between the vector values and the label.

Jlens fitted on LLMail

For the first lens, we use an existing Jacobian lens fitted on 100 raw LLMail prompts, with a maximum fitting length of 128 tokens. It converts an intermediate hidden state vector into estimated vocabulary scores, which we then use to compare the injection and safe labels.

Jlens fitted on synthetic office prompts

The second lens was fitted on 100 curated synthetic office prompts, again with a maximum fitting length of 128 tokens, but these prompts include an explicit classification instruction. Both lenses then select examples from FIPI, so LLMail and synthetic office prompts describe the lens-fitting data, not the selected fine-tuning data.

The shared selection score

For the three probes and two lenses, we use the same selection formula. A high score means the detector assigned little probability to the correct label, so a confidently incorrect prediction receives priority.

\begin{aligned} s(x,y) &= 1 - p(y \mid x) \\ S(\text{pair}) &= \frac{s(x_{\mathrm{injection}},\mathrm{injection}) + s(x_{\mathrm{safe}},\mathrm{safe})}{2} \end{aligned}
x is a prompt, y is its known label, and p is the detector's probability for that label

According to the figure above (Figure 2), the detector assigns 90% to safe for an injection prompt, and 3% to injection for its safe counterpart. Averaging these incorrect-label probabilities gives a pair selection score of (0.90 + 0.03) / 2 = 0.465, so both prompts affect which pairs are selected. We rank eligible pairs by this score and retain the highest 256.

The 1,500 training pairs and 500 validation pairs are separate from the candidate source groups. Random selection, text diversity, and model failures have their own rules; their numerical ranking scores should not be interpreted as incorrect-label probabilities.

How Jlens turns a hidden state vector into words

A transformer layer produces a hidden state vector, or a list of numbers that represents the prompt at that point in the model. Jlens gives us a way to read that vector through the model's vocabulary. Gurnee et al. introduced the Jacobian lens to identify concepts that a model can verbalize from its internal representations. [11]

For each fitting prompt, the model first runs a forward pass. Automatic differentiation then measures how a small change in an earlier hidden state value affects values at the final layer. Collecting these derivatives forms a Jacobian matrix. Our fitting procedure combines derivatives over selected token positions and averages across the fitting prompts, giving one matrix for each inspected layer while the model's own weights remain fixed.

At inference time, the saved matrix J_l is multiplied by the hidden state vector h_l at layer l. This gives the lens's estimate of the final hidden state vector. The readout then applies the model's final RMS normalization and unembedding matrix to produce vocabulary scores, or logits.

\begin{aligned} \hat{h}_{\mathrm{final}} &= J_l h_l \\ z &= W_U\,\operatorname{RMSNorm}(\hat{h}_{\mathrm{final}}) \end{aligned}
J_l is the fitted Jacobian matrix, W_U is the unembedding matrix, and z contains one logit per vocabulary token

According to the figure above (Figure 3), the hidden state vector first passes through the Jacobian and final normalization before the unembedding matrix gives us token logits. Softmax then converts those logits into probabilities, where a higher logit produces a larger exponential while the denominator is shared. For selection, we restrict this comparison to the two label tokens:

\begin{aligned} p(\mathrm{injection} \mid x) &= \frac{\exp(z_{\mathrm{injection}})}{\exp(z_{\mathrm{injection}}) + \exp(z_{\mathrm{safe}})} \\ p(\mathrm{safe} \mid x) &= 1 - p(\mathrm{injection} \mid x) \end{aligned}

For example, logits of 2 and 0 produce an injection probability of about 88.1% when softmax is restricted to those two labels. This worked example therefore describes the lens's preference between injection and safe, rather than the probability of generating that label among every vocabulary token.

The selection run reads the hidden state vector at the last prompt token, before the response begins. It does not search a generated answer for a label or force every intermediate layer to verbalize one. The earlier reasoning-enabled layer inspections are a separate experiment, available in the interactive visualization.

Both lenses use the same validation rule as the probes: choose the layer with the highest mean of injection accuracy and safe accuracy. That layer stays fixed when scoring all candidate prompts.

The training objective

Initially, we explored hidden state separation objectives inspired by ODILE, then compared separation alone, reference-response training alone, and their combination on the same FIPI pairs. [8]

The moving-average injection center vector

For each training example, we average the adapted model's hidden state vectors across the first 50 response tokens, or fewer if the response is shorter. Each inspected layer has its own center vector, initialized from the first example's average. Later updates combine 90% of the previous center with 10% of the new average, so the center gradually changes during training. The injection loss pulls the adapted hidden state vectors toward this center and away from the original model's vectors. The safe loss keeps them close to the original safe-response vectors and away from the injection center.

According to the figure above (Figure 4), the center keeps most of its previous value while moving toward the current response average. Hence, it is a moving average rather than an equally weighted average of every example. The averaging rule updates the center directly, while the separation losses guide changes to the LoRA parameters that produce the hidden state vectors.

These two losses compare distances between hidden state vectors, without directly checking whether the next token belongs to a correct task answer. We therefore also tested reference-response training, which gives the model a token-by-token answer to learn.

Learning the safe counterpart response

For reference-response training, the base model first answers the safe version of a prompt, and we keep that generated answer as the reference. During fine-tuning, the adapter receives the injected version and learns to produce this reference response token by token.

According to the figure above (Figure 5), both versions share the same legitimate task, so the safe prompt supplies the answer that the injected prompt should still produce. During training, the model receives the preceding reference tokens, a procedure called teacher forcing, and learns to predict the next one, including the end-of-response token.

We use cross-entropy loss to compare the model's next-token probabilities with the reference token at each response position. A smaller probability for that reference token gives a larger loss, so training rewards the reference answer under the injected input.

L = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}\!\left(y_t^{\mathrm{safe}} \mid x^{\mathrm{injection}}, y_{<t}^{\mathrm{safe}}\right)
T is the number of reference tokens, y_t^{\mathrm{safe}} is the current reference token, and \theta contains the trainable LoRA parameters

According to the figure above (Figure 6), assigning the reference token probability 0.10 gives a loss of -\log(0.10), about 2.30, whereas probability 0.80 gives a loss of about 0.22. Training therefore rewards a higher probability for the reference answer, with loss applied to answer tokens rather than prompt tokens.

In the rewriting example, this means learning to simplify the passage without answering the inserted question. The final objective therefore trains the response behavior directly, without a separate hidden state alignment loss or a one-word injection/safe classification target.

Training on injected inputs with legitimate-task answers also appears in StruQ, which combines instruction tuning with a front end that separates trusted instructions from supplied data. Here we start from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and compare which examples to select for LoRA training. [17]

We use LoRA to learn small matrix updates while keeping the original model weights fixed. For a selected weight matrix W, the effective weight becomes W + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA, where A and B are the smaller trainable matrices, r is their rank, and \alpha sets their scale. [12]

What the evaluations test

Finally, we evaluate the base model and eight LoRA adapters across eight setups. Nine model versions means one base model plus eight adapters. Selection methods name the training data used by an adapter; they are not detectors running in front of the model during these evaluations.

FIPI held-out testing data

We use 200 testing pairs with legitimate tasks and safe/injected content, checking whether the injected response contains the answer requested by the inserted instruction. Since these pairs come from FIPI, they test new examples from the training dataset rather than a new data source. [10]

LLMail

LLMail contains prompts designed to manipulate an assistant that reads and summarizes messages. We adapt 1,000 injection prompts and 110 safe prompts to one shared summary task, then check for an unauthorized send_email request without executing it. Hence, this tests the adapted task rather than reproducing the original retrieval challenge. [2]

Synthetic business

We use 400 safe/injection pairs based on synthetic office requests, such as preparing service-change records. Within simulated business tasks, we check whether the model discloses a case-specific fake secret or carries out injected changes to protected request fields, including changes written into the text of an accepted record.

BIPIA

BIPIA tests tasks that use external content, with instructions inserted to alter the output. Our subset covers message, table, and code tasks with published testing injections at the beginning, middle, and end of content. We combine published rules, a local model judge, and recorded review decisions, although this setup neither executes generated code nor covers the entire BIPIA task collection. [13]

AgentDyn

AgentDyn provides shopping, GitHub, and daily-life environments with 60 legitimate tasks and 560 injection cases. Tool calls execute inside simulated environments, where original task-specific checks determine whether the attacker goal occurred, alongside separate checks for legitimate-task completion. [14]

AgentLAB

AgentLAB studies attacks across extended agent interactions, and we use its released Task-Injection examples in workplace, travel, banking, and messaging environments. We replay the first available released candidate per task pair with the original environment checks, so this evaluates fixed attacks rather than the full adaptive attacker or the other AgentLAB attack families. [15]

InjecAgent

InjecAgent combines user tasks, tool descriptions, and simulated tool responses carrying injected instructions. We test 2,108 cases across its base and enhanced settings, checking whether the model requests an attacker-directed tool. Some data-theft cases need another model response after a simulated tool result, although the measured outcome remains the requested action rather than verified real-world execution. [16]

AgentDojo

AgentDojo provides multi-step tasks in workplace, travel, banking, and messaging environments. We use a fixed published injection template, with tool execution and subsequent responses occurring in the simulated environment, then apply the original checks for attacker goals. This therefore compares fixed attacks rather than an attacker that optimizes against every adapter. [1]

Within each evaluation, the system instructions stay the same across model versions, although they differ between benchmarks because the tasks and tool protocols differ. FIPI testing uses greedy generation with a 1,024-token response limit, while the external setups retain their separately recorded generation and tool-turn limits.

Findings

Jlens-selected data performed well beyond FIPI

On held-out FIPI prompts, all eight adapters reduced the injected-answer rate from the base model's 52.5% to between 0.5% and 2.0%. Logistic Regression and Jlens fitted on LLMail tied at 0.5%, or one response out of 200.

The differences between selection methods are larger on the external datasets, where Jlens fitted on LLMail produced the lowest observed ASR on adapted LLMail, synthetic business tasks, AgentDyn, AgentLAB, and InjecAgent. For example, the adapted LLMail result fell from 39.3% for the base model to 6.2% for randomly selected training data and 2.8% for LLMail-lens-selected data.

Nevertheless, BIPIA favored the 2 layer neural network at 8.35%, compared with 9.68% for either lens, while AgentDojo favored Linear SVM at 1.18%, compared with 4.78% for Jlens fitted on LLMail and 5.43% for Jlens fitted on synthetic prompts. Hence, the current results favor Jlens on most tested benchmarks without showing that it is always the best selector, especially since the comparison uses one model and training seed.

According to the figure above (Figure 16), the probes classified these validation prompts much more accurately than either lens, yet their selected training data did not consistently produce the lowest external ASR. Hence, a detector that performs well at classification does not necessarily select the most useful examples for fine-tuning.

One possibility is that the lenses prioritize examples that the probes already classify correctly, which we could test by comparing the selected subsets. For now, the ASR results alone cannot explain why those examples helped, and the two-label lens probabilities have not been calibrated against observed classification accuracy.

What the earlier verbalization experiments add

The earlier experiments started from interpreting model layers, where we observed examples of an intermediate Jlens readout favoring injection while the model's final label favored safe. This motivated the later question of whether those layer readouts could help select useful fine-tuning examples.

Both lenses used here were fitted on 100 prompts, but this alone does not make either lens a reliable injection judge. The neutral FIPI selection prompt gives them only 53.3% and 53.9% validation accuracy at their selected layer. Classification-formatted inspection prompts ask a different question and must be assessed separately.

Our two fitting methods also differ in both dataset and prompt format. To determine whether inference instructions matter more than fitting data, we would need to vary one factor at a time. For now, the supported observation is that prompt format changes the readout, and that these readouts can still be useful for selecting training data.

Limitations and next experiments

The current comparison focuses on attack success rate, so it does not establish that legitimate multi-turn agent tasks remain complete and correct amidst an injection attack. Since we fine-tuned on selected FIPI task/content pairs rather than multi-turn tool-use trajectories, a separate task-performance experiment is still necessary before making a deployment claim.

In this project, FIPI supplies paired inputs for response training, with the base model's safe-prompt answers used as references. Those answers may contain mistakes because their task correctness was not systematically verified, so reviewing reference-answer quality is another part of data curation worth testing.

The external results also use benchmark adaptations, and some cases remain unresolved because the available responses do not support a reliable score. The count tables therefore show each model's scored denominator, while the synthetic-business and BIPIA reviews still require a second review because they used Codex assistance rather than independent human annotation.

The LLMail lens already had exposure to LLMail during fitting. Its adapter was fine-tuned only on selected FIPI pairs, but the overall selection pipeline is therefore not completely unfamiliar with LLMail. A strict unseen-dataset comparison would fit the selector without that source and hold the evaluation dataset out from every stage.

The next experiments would expand the comparison in several directions:

  • More models. Repeat the same selection comparison across model families and sizes to test whether the result extends beyond Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.
  • More training sources. Select batches from datasets beyond FIPI, including task trajectories with tool use, then compare FIPI-only training with mixtures of sources while keeping testing data separate.
  • Legitimate task completion. Measure whether the requested task succeeds in both safe and injected conditions alongside ASR, using identical tool handling for every model.
  • Repeated selections and training runs. Repeat seeds and compare selection sizes such as 256, 512, and 1,024 pairs, while grouping related prompt variants when estimating uncertainty.
  • Why the selected examples help. Inspect the overlap between selected subsets, their injection categories, and reference-answer quality, then compare the current scores with a gradient-based selector such as LESS. [9]
  • Adaptive attacks. Evaluate attacks that know the adapter and can revise their prompts, rather than relying only on fixed released examples.

The immediate experiment is to keep the response-training objective fixed, vary the data source and selection rule, and evaluate both injection resistance and legitimate task completion on held-out environments.

Subliminal learning and synthetic training data

Another question is whether model-generated training data can pass on behaviors beyond the task being taught. Cloud et al. show that a student can acquire a teacher's preferences after fine-tuning on its number sequences, code, or mathematical reasoning, even after references to those preferences are removed. Their results depend on a shared or closely matched starting model, rather than simply having the same number of parameters. This raises a further data-curation question: what might a model learn from synthetic data that looks harmless when inspected? [18]

For our office-scenario dataset, the archived generator used existing business prompts and requested standalone injection instructions from an LLM, then inserted those instructions locally to form injection and safe pairs. The complete prompts were therefore not generated from scratch, and the retained records do not identify the exact generator checkpoint. These pairs supported detector fitting and testing, while the final eight LoRA adapters were trained on selected FIPI pairs. Fitting Jlens also left the language model's weights unchanged.

The closer connection is our reference-response training, since Qwen generated the safe-prompt answers that adapters on the same base checkpoint then learned to reproduce under injection. This makes teacher behavior worth investigating, although we have not tested whether any unrelated preference was transferred. Learning to complete the intended task is the training goal; a change in an unrelated preference would require a separate evaluation.

A follow-up experiment could compare reference answers from a neutral teacher with answers from the same teacher prompted to prefer an unrelated animal, while excluding any answers that mention or imply that preference. With the FIPI inputs, answer-quality criteria, training budget, and student checkpoint held constant, we could compare random selection with Jlens and probe selection, then measure changes in the student's preference alongside ASR and legitimate task completion. Repeating this with a teacher from another model family would test whether the relationship between teacher and student matters. For future datasets, retaining the generator checkpoint, generation instructions, and sampling settings would make this comparison traceable, while filtering visible content alone would not establish that unrelated behaviors cannot transfer.
